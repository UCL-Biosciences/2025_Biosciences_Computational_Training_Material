# Biosciences Computational Training - General Course Intro

## Background
We will discuss a lot of stuff over the five workshops. Why should we bother with any of this stuff? There are lots of really good reasons!

### Intro and group activity - why do we need computational skills?
1. Group Discussion. Aim to get thinking about challenges in research and what they want from the course. No reporting as we will use menti
- What are the main challenges holding up your research?
- Which if any could be improved by increasing computational knowledge/skills?

### Why learn?
Some general thoughts.

**Computational skills are no longer niche or exclusively for specialist; we all must have some of these skills**
- Skills more important than ever – not just for doing niche stats or bioinformatics. Papers want open source code and reproducible results, grant bodies want clear commitment to FAIR principles, understand fundamentals of AI to enjoy benefits.
- Close gap between “computational” and “non-computational” people. That distinction is gone – all must do comp work and no reason we can’t all do it to a high standard

**The knowledge and skills are accessible. No reason to avoid it or let it be gatekept**
- Comp bio skills are not magic – easier than ever to learn. This course will introduce key concepts and good practice to support that learning
- Lots of researchers rely on collaborators for computational work. We should enjoy collaborating with specialists, while also having the knowledge to understand what collabs do. At least enough to evaluate what they have done.

**Your work will be better and you will be happier**
- Confidence – that you can trace what you’ve done, that it is correct, and that if not, you can identify mistakes and correct them easily.
- Code, results, data that is FAIR are more likely to be used by others. More citations, but also means your effort isn’t wasted and we don’t keep starting from scratch every time we do an analysis!

## Aims for the course**
By the end of this course, participants will:
1.	See computational skills as a normal, expected part of biological research — not a specialist add-on — and understand why the field now requires this of everyone (papers, funders, and basic scientific practice).
2.	Have hands-on experience with the core tools of computational biology — Python, git/GitHub, environment management, HPC, and a practical grounding in AI/ML — enough to continue learning independently and know where to go deeper.
3.	Have a starting knowledge for how to work reproducibly and manage research outputs well (FAIR) — so their own work is traceable, correct, and checkable, and so their code, data, and results can be understood and reused by others.
4.	Leave with a working project template — spanning data → analysis → figures → version control → environment → HPC — that they can reuse and extend in their own research.
